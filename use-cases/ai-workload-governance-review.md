# AI Workload Governance Review

> **HRMC relationship:** Adjacent research, not a current HRMC product capability.  
> **Evidence level:** Proposed workflow pattern; not production-validated.

## Summary

Helps HR and People leaders inspect whether AI adoption is reducing work friction or quietly turning into higher workload expectations.

The workflow does not ask, "Are people using AI?"

It asks, "What changed about capacity, priorities, manager behavior, review burden, and employee sustainability after AI entered the workflow?"

## What problem does this solve?

AI adoption often starts as enablement.

Then the organization sees faster drafts, more tickets, more analysis, more variants, and shorter cycle times. If leaders do not reset priorities and capacity assumptions, the productivity gain can become permanent intensity.

Employees may not fear replacement first. They may fear being expected to do more for the same pay, with less slack, more parallel work, and more review burden.

## What does this workflow do?

- Reviews how AI tools changed actual team workload.
- Separates adoption metrics from sustainability signals.
- Checks whether managers made explicit priority tradeoffs.
- Tracks whether AI created more human review work.
- Flags teams where output expectations rose without capacity discussion.
- Creates manager questions for HRBP follow-up.
- Preserves human review and decision ownership.

## Who uses it / who is affected?

- HRBPs / People Business Partners
- Product and Engineering leaders
- Design, Research, Risk, and Support-adjacent teams
- Managers adopting AI tools
- Employees whose workload, priorities, or review burden changed

## What is the real-world impact?

- **More structured AI governance** — supports review of work design, not tool usage alone.
- **Clearer manager questions** — prompts leaders to name priority tradeoffs and stop-doing decisions.
- **Earlier workload-risk visibility** — may surface pressure signals for human interpretation.
- **More explicit capacity planning** — challenges the assumption that saved time is automatically free capacity.
- **Bounded HR partnership** — supports sustainable operating discussions without making automated conclusions.

## What data does it need?

Use the least sensitive data that can answer the question.

Good inputs:

- team-level AI adoption patterns
- aggregate workload and capacity signals
- manager priority lists
- project intake and backlog trends
- review queue volume
- engagement or pulse themes at aggregate level
- meeting load and cycle-time trends
- manager follow-up notes

Avoid:

- individual productivity surveillance
- keystroke or browser activity monitoring
- private employee prompts
- sensitive employee relations notes
- confidential performance details outside an approved environment

## What must stay human-reviewed?

Human review is required for:

- interpreting burnout or engagement signals
- deciding staffing or priority tradeoffs
- changing performance expectations
- making compensation, promotion, discipline, or termination decisions
- introducing employee monitoring
- drawing conclusions about named employees or managers

The workflow may surface signals and questions.

It should not decide whether a team, manager, or employee is "healthy," "at risk," or "underperforming."

## Review questions

Use these questions with leaders:

- Which work got faster because of AI?
- What work expanded because AI made more output possible?
- What did we stop doing?
- Where did human review load increase?
- Are managers making tradeoffs explicit, or just absorbing more work?
- Are employees using AI to reduce friction or to survive higher expectations?
- What would we expect to see if the team were burning out quietly?
- Which capacity assumption should be revisited this month?

## What would make it demo-ready?

- Use a fully synthetic Product/Engineering team at ExampleCo.
- Show before/after signals: priorities, review queues, cycle time, meeting load, and employee pulse themes.
- Generate a one-page review packet.
- Include manager questions, missing facts, and human-review boundaries.
- End with a decision log: what changed, who owns the next conversation, and what should not be automated.

The point is not to make HR anti-AI.

The point is to make AI adoption honest about workload, capacity, and human judgment.
